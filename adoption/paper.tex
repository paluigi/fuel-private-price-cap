\title{Counterfactual Prices, Inflation, and Adoption:\\
A Voluntary Price Cap in the Italian Retail Fuel Market}
\author{Working paper --- draft \today}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent When international oil prices were rising steeply, Italy's largest
fuel retailer voluntarily capped its pump prices from 28 September 2026 at a
nominal 1.99~\euro/l for petrol and 2.19~\euro/l for diesel, and most of the
market followed within weeks. We reconstruct the effective daily price of
every roadside Italian fuel station from the official price-communication
registry, strip the statutory tax calendar, and compare the observed price
path with a no-cap counterfactual built from pre-cap oil pass-through
estimated over January--September 2026. The counterfactual projects October
pump prices 17.4~\cl{} (petrol) and 21.1~\cl{} (diesel) above realised
prices on average, a range that survives credible specification and
forecast-validation checks. Evaluated at the statistical office's reference
days, the cap turned fuel inflation from $+5.3$ and $+10.9\%$ (petrol and
diesel, no cap) into $-2.3$ and $+1.6\%$, an estimated contribution of about
$-0.4$ percentage points to October headline consumer-price inflation. The
station-level panel explains how the aggregate effect arose: the leader cut
most, branded rivals matched within days, prices bunched one tenth of a cent
below the ceiling, and the 28 October diesel excise cut was passed through
only gradually at the ceiling. A time-of-day reconstruction correction of up
to 5~\cl{} matters for reference-day price measurement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Between January and October 2026 the price of Brent crude in euro doubled,
from roughly 53 to 108~\euro{} per barrel. In the middle of this surge, on
25 September 2026, Eni---the company operating Italy's largest fuel-retail
network---announced that from the following Monday, 28 September, it would
cap the self-service prices at its stations at 1.99~\euro{} per litre of
petrol and 2.19~\euro{} per litre of diesel, roughly 17 cents below the
prevailing market level, initially for thirty days with a possible extension
to the end of the year. Rival networks matched within days and the
commitment on the cap level was maintained, by the company's
communications, through the end of October, the window we study. A
voluntary, firm-initiated price ceiling during an inflationary oil shock is
an unusual event, and it poses a well-defined question: how much lower were
Italian pump prices than they would otherwise have been, and what did that
difference contribute to measured consumer-price inflation?

Answering that question requires measuring three things. First, the
\emph{observed} national price path, which we construct from the complete
registry of Italian station price communications---12.4 million
station--fuel--day observations covering 21{,}766 stations over
2026---reconstructing for every station and day the price actually in force
rather than the price recorded by the daily extract. Second, a
\emph{conditional no-cap path}: an econometric model of how national
net-of-tax pump prices passed through the Brent price before the cap,
projected over the cap window on the actual oil path with the actual
statutory taxes reapplied. The counterfactual is conditional on the
maintained assumption that pre-cap pass-through would have continued absent
the cap; it is not an experimentally identified quantity, and we
discipline it with forecast validation and specification bounds rather
than claim identification. Third, an \emph{inflation-accounting
implication}: the difference between the observed and no-cap paths,
evaluated on the 1st, 11th and 21st of September and October---the
reference days on which Italy's statistical office (ISTAT) collects fuel
prices for the consumer-price index---and aggregated with explicit,
documented assumptions.

Three findings emerge. The observed effect is large: averaging over the 32
post-cap days for which price extracts exist, the no-cap model projects
October prices 17.4~\cl{} above realised prices for petrol and
21.1~\cl{} for diesel; on 31 October the gaps are 22.2 and 20.4~\cl.
Because prices are collected on reference days, this price difference maps
into measured inflation: month-on-month fuel prices fell 2.3\% (petrol) and
rose 1.6\% (diesel) with the cap, against a no-cap counterfactual of $+5.3$
and $+10.9\%$, an estimated contribution of $-0.38$ percentage points to
October headline inflation (specification range $-0.38$ to $-0.45$). The
propagation is leader-led: on the first day 21\% of stations priced below
the ceiling, half within four days, and 83--84\% at some point by
31 October; the initiator's network accounted for a disproportionate share
of the total price decline, unbranded independents the smallest. And the
ceiling reshaped the price distribution: a large mass of stations priced
one tenth of a cent below it, the ninetieth--tenth percentile spread of
within-province prices rose by about seventy per cent, and when a diesel
excise cut shifted the ceiling down on 28 October the network passed the
tax change through only gradually---a small natural experiment in
ceiling-constrained tax incidence.

Our contribution is primarily one of measurement and interpretation of an
aggregate policy episode, and the paper speaks to four literatures. To the
large body of work on oil-to-retail pass-through and asymmetric pricing
\parencite{tappata2009, atil2013, borenstein2002, bettendorf2003}, we add a
case in which pass-through was suspended not by regulation of margins but
by a firm's own price commitment. To work on price ceilings in retail fuel
markets---statutory ceilings, their focal-point effects and their
interaction with market structure
\parencite{carranza2015, sen2011, zhang2020, contin1999, gatsios2026,
quiguanas2025}---we add a voluntary, leader-initiated ceiling with
near-complete market matching, observed at daily frequency. To the
literature on price leadership and price matching
\parencite{atkinson2008, amir2007, kim2019, pellegrino2025}, we provide a
clean episode in which the follower response can be observed station by
station. Finally, to work on price-index measurement with administrative
data \parencite{rossi2015, pennerstorfer2020}, we show that reconstructing
prices by communication time rather than extract time corrects
reference-day averages by up to 5~\cl{} on high-churn days, a distinction
that matters for any statistics built on the Italian registry.

The paper proceeds as follows. \Cref{sec:setting} describes the
institutional setting, including the 2026 excise calendar that any analysis
of Italian fuel prices must respect. \Cref{sec:data} documents the data and
the effective-price reconstruction. \Cref{sec:observed} presents the
observed market response. \Cref{sec:counterfactual} builds and validates
the no-cap counterfactual; \Cref{sec:inflation} translates it into an
estimated inflation contribution. \Cref{sec:mechanism} examines how the
response propagated across stations, brands and geography.
\Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Institutional setting}\label{sec:setting}

Italian road fuel is sold through roughly 21{,}000 reporting roadside
(\emph{stradale}) stations under a mix of brand banners---Eni's network,
the other large brands (Q8, Api-Ip, Eni's former competitors Esso and
Tamoil among them)---and unbranded ``white pump'' independents, alongside
some 2{,}000 motorway stations whose prices are regulated at higher levels
and which we exclude throughout. Since 2005 every price change must be
communicated to the Ministry of Enterprises (MIMIT), which publishes the
full registry daily as open data; the price in force at any moment is
therefore a matter of public record. Italy's consumer-price statistics
draw fuel prices from this same registry, observing them on three
reference days per month---the 1st, the 11th and the 21st.

Two features of the 2026 tax calendar are essential context. First, excise
duty was itself on a roller-coaster: a 20~\cl{} cut for both fuels took
effect on 19 March, and the discount was phased back in through a sequence
of steps between 2 May and 4 July, with a further diesel-only discount
from 28 July that was itself withdrawn in steps on 18 and 26 September,
days before the cap. Second, the cap was explicitly framed by the
announcing company as linked to the excise relief in force: when the last
5~\cl{} of diesel discount was withdrawn on 6 October, the gross ceiling
mechanically translated upward with it, and when a 4~\cl{} diesel excise
cut took effect on 28 October the ceiling translated downward. Any
compliance or price-gap measure must therefore use a date-specific
ceiling, which we construct below. Petrol excise was unchanged after
4 July; in particular there is no petrol tax change in the outcome window.
Value-added tax is 22\% throughout, so a one-cent excise change moves the
gross pump price by 1.22 cents.

The company's announcement (25 September, effective 28 September) stated
nominal ceilings of 1.99 and 2.19~\euro/l and an initial duration of
thirty days, with extension to year-end possible depending on market
conditions. Prices in the registry are quoted to three decimals. Our
operational compliance cutoffs are the rounded-up gross levels of
2.000~\euro/l (petrol) and 2.200~\euro/l (diesel): a price of 1.999 or
2.199 complies. We apply the cutoff strictly---a price exactly at
2.000 or 2.200 does not comply---which is empirically nearly inconsequential:
exact-boundary observations never exceed 0.06\% of station-days
(\cref{tab:boundary}). The diesel ceiling steps to 2.261 on 6 October
(2.200 plus the withdrawn 5~\cl{} excise grossed up by VAT) and to 2.212
on 28 October (2.261 minus the 4~\cl{} cut grossed up). Throughout, the
analysis window ends on 31 October, by which date the hard commitment on
the cap level had run its course; communications about pricing beyond that
date were less specific, and we do not analyse them.

\section{Data and measurement}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources}

We use four sources. The MIMIT price registry, as daily extracts covering
1 January to 5 November 2026: 307 extract days (16--17 October were never
published, the only gap) and 12.4 million rows for self-service petrol and
diesel. Weekly station snapshots provide each station's banner, operator,
type and location. The statutory excise calendar, obtained from the
historical tax registry back to 2005, gives every application date and
rate. Brent in euro is the FRED Brent spot price converted at the
USD/euro reference rate, forward-filled to calendar days.
\Cref{tab:data} summarises the resulting samples.

\subsection{From communications to effective prices}

The registry extract published on a given day contains, for every station
and fuel, the last communicated price---and each row carries the timestamp
at which that price was communicated. The extract therefore mixes prices
set at very different times, and on high-churn days it can misrepresent
the price in force on any particular day. The problem is sharpest exactly
where it matters for us: the cap was implemented through a wave of
communications in the early morning of 28 September, so the extract
published that day still shows, for most stations, the pre-cap price,
while the extract of 1 October straddles the following repricing wave.

We therefore reconstruct the \emph{effective} price of every station--fuel
pair on every day: the price of the last communication dated on or before
that day. A station enters the panel on its first in-window communication
and leaves when it stops being observed in extracts, so stale prices are
never carried forward indefinitely. The practical stakes are large. On
1 October 2026 the mean extract price exceeded the mean effective price by
4.2~\cl{} (petrol) and 4.9~\cl{} (diesel), because the morning extract
cut off mid-wave; a reference-day statistic built on extract-time prices
would overstate that day's price by that amount. All our price series are
effective-price series; we return to the measurement implications in
\Cref{sec:inflation}. The only gap in the reconstructed daily path is
16--17 October, when no extracts exist at all; we never interpolate it,
and averages over the cap window are over the 32 available days of 34.

\subsection{From pump prices to net-of-tax prices}

For the counterfactual we work with the net-of-tax price, defined as
\[
p^{net}_{st} = \frac{p_{st}}{1+v} - \tau_t,
\]
where $p_{st}$ is the effective gross price at station $s$ on day $t$,
$v = 0.22$ is the VAT rate and $\tau_t$ the statutory excise for that fuel
on day $t$. Netting the tax removes every 2026 excise step---the spring
cut, its phased reinstatement, the pre-cap diesel steps and the two
post-cap diesel steps---from both sides of the pass-through relationship
by construction, so no tax dummy belongs in the model. The net-of-tax
price is a price received along the supply chain, not a retail margin:
the wedge between it and the wholesale cost is shared between refining,
distribution and retailing, and we make no claim about that split. The
national series we analyse are equal-weighted means of effective prices
across roadside self-service stations, about 19{,}700 per fuel per day,
with a balanced-panel variant (stations present on at least 95\% of days)
and an ISTAT-like regularity-filtered variant (stations that communicated
at least forty distinct price changes over July--September, 57.5\% of
units) used as composition checks.

\subsection{Compliance and adoption}

A station--fuel pair is defined as pricing below the ceiling on day $t$ if
its effective gross price is strictly below the date-specific ceiling of
\Cref{sec:setting}. Three distinct objects should not be confused: the
share of observations below the ceiling on a given day; the share of
units that have \emph{ever} priced below it since 28 September (our
adoption measure); and formal enrolment in any company's initiative,
which we do not observe and do not require---pricing below the ceiling is
a behaviour, not a membership. The estimation panel for adoption
comprises 39{,}423 station--fuel units observed from 28 September to
31 October (4.7 million station-days), of which 32{,}971 adopt at some
point.

\Cref{fig:prices} shows the national price paths in their 2026 context.
The spring excise cut pulled both fuels down by roughly 20~\cl{} in
March; the phased reinstatement lifted them through the summer while oil
was already rising; and at the moment of the cap in late September diesel
stood at 2.374~\euro/l and petrol at 2.158~\euro/l, both close to their
yearly highs. Over the cap window, petrol fell to 2.009 by 31 October
($-14.9$~\cl) and diesel to 2.239 ($-13.5$~\cl). The diesel path is the
harder to read, because two excise steps fall inside the window: the
6 October increase (+6.1~\cl{} gross) and the 28 October cut
($-4.9$~\cl{} gross). In net-of-tax terms---which strips both---petrol
fell 12.2 and diesel 12.1~\cl{} over the window, against a Brent price
that rose further.

Adoption was fast and broad. On the first day, 20.9\% of petrol and
21.4\% of diesel observations were already priced below the ceiling; by
the fourth day (2 October) roughly half were; by 31 October 84.4\% of
petrol units and 82.8\% of diesel units had ever priced below their
date-specific ceiling, with a median first-compliance day of 4. Two
compositional notes are essential. First, final-day and ever-shares have
different denominators: on 31 October itself, 81.8\% of petrol and 50.2\%
of diesel \emph{observations} were below the ceiling, the latter lower
because the corrected 2.212 ceiling of the last three days is harder to
meet, as \Cref{sec:mechanism} shows. Second, no measurable anticipation
preceded implementation: in the event-study of \Cref{sec:mechanism} the
pre-cap coefficients for 21--27 September are between $-0.2$ and
$-0.4$ percentage points, an order of magnitude smaller than the
19-point jump on day zero, and the announcement-to-implementation gap of
25--27 September shows no break in the aggregate price path.

Brand groups ordered the response exactly as a leadership account would
predict. Eni's network was at the ceiling almost immediately and stayed
there; Q8 and Api-Ip matched within a week; Esso and Tamoil followed
slightly later; and unbranded independents converged only partially.
A simple descriptive decomposition of the 27 September--31 October mean
price decline (\cref{fig:mech}a) attributes 27\% of the diesel decline to
Eni's network---which accounts for about 19\% of observations---and only
4\% to white pumps, whose prices fell least. The 6 October diesel episode
separates mechanics from behaviour cleanly: of the 48-point jump in the
share of diesel observations inside the newly compliant band
[2.200,~2.261), 43 points are stations that actively re-communicated a
price that day; only 5 points are stations whose pre-existing price
merely found itself inside the widened band. The compliance jump was
overwhelmingly repricing, not reclassification.

\section{A no-cap price counterfactual}\label{sec:counterfactual}

\subsection{Strategy}

The counterfactual question is what national prices would have been over
28 September--31 October had the cap not happened, given the oil path that
actually occurred. Our answer conditions on the pre-cap relationship
between net-of-tax pump prices and the Brent price in euro, estimated on
the extended window from 2 January to 27 September 2026---261 daily
observations spanning the spring excise cut, its reinstatement and the
pre-cap oil surge, all of which are absorbed by the net-of-tax
construction. The maintained assumption is that this pass-through
relationship would have continued; everything that follows is an attempt
to make that assumption transparent, to test where it can be tested, and
to bound the conclusions where it cannot.

\subsection{Model and selection}

For each fuel we estimate an autoregressive distributed-lag (ARDL) model
in logs,
\[
\ln p^{net}_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{q}\phi_i \ln p^{net}_{t-i}
+ \sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j \ln b_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t,
\]
where $b_t$ is Brent in euro and lag orders are selected by the Bayesian
information criterion over an exhaustive grid: own lags $p \in [0,21]$ and
oil lags $q \in [7,21]$, with the floor of seven oil lags imposing at
least one week of Brent dynamics in the polynomial. The floor reflects a
substantive prior---retail prices adjust to oil over days, not
overnight---and we verify below that the results are insensitive to it.
On the extended window, BIC selects $(p,q) = (2,8)$ for petrol and
$(1,8)$ for diesel (\cref{tab:ardl}).

\subsection{Validation}

Three exercises discipline the model. First, a placebo backtest: refit on
data through 13 September and project recursively over 14--27 September,
still pre-cap. The model attains a root-mean-square error of 0.45~\cl{}
(petrol) and 0.85~\cl{} (diesel) on the net price, against 3.69 and
5.07~\cl{} for a no-change random walk, with biases near zero
(\cref{tab:ardl}). Second, expanding-origin backtests: five origins from
31 May to 13 September, projecting 14 and 28 days ahead, all evaluation
windows strictly pre-cap. The oil model beats the random walk in eleven
of sixteen origin--horizon cells; the losses concentrate at 14-day
horizons from the mid-summer origins, when prices drifted with little oil
news. We report these losses rather than select around them; the origins
closest to the counterfactual period---late August and September---favour
the oil model decisively. Third, block-exclusion tests with
heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-robust covariance reject the
exclusion of the Brent block for both fuels ($p < 10^{-10}$). A pure
autoregression without oil terms is reported as a mechanical persistence
benchmark: on the extended window it forecasts \emph{worse} than the oil
models for both fuels (backtest 3.00 and 4.31~\cl), which was not true on
the shorter summer-only window and is one reason we prefer the extended
estimation sample.

One diagnostic deserves honest statement. Both selected specifications
carry near-unit-root persistence (the sum of own-lag coefficients is
0.987 for petrol and 0.995 for diesel), so the implied long-run
elasticity of the diesel main specification---0.09---is a
small-denominator artifact and not a structural estimate. Across the
admissible oil-lag floors, long-run elasticities range from 0.72 to 0.77
for petrol and 0.81 to 0.94 for diesel. The credibility of the
counterfactual rests on its validated forecasting behaviour, not on a
point estimate of long-run pass-through.

\Cref{fig:cf} presents the projection. To obtain a no-cap \emph{gross}
price we re-apply the statutory tax calendar to the projected net
path---the same excise on the same days as in the observed series---so
the 6 and 28 October diesel tax steps affect both paths identically. The
counterfactual petrol price starts where the market started, about
3~\cl{} above the realised price on 28 September, and climbs with Brent
to 2.232~\euro/l by 31 October, 22.2~\cl{} above the actual price; the
diesel counterfactual reaches 2.444, a gap of 20.4~\cl. Averaged over
the window, the no-cap path lies 17.4~\cl{} (petrol) and 21.1~\cl{}
(diesel) above the realised one. On the inflation reference days the gaps
are $-9.5$, $-19.3$ and $-19.3$~\cl{} (petrol; 1/11/21 October) and
$-11.2$, $-27.8$ and $-22.9$~\cl{} (diesel).

Uncertainty is reported in layers, each covering a distinct component.
Residual-shock bootstrap bands (2{,}000 draws) reflect innovation
uncertainty conditional on the estimated coefficients; moving-block
bootstrap bands (block length seven) add serial dependence in the
shocks; a re-estimation bootstrap (400 draws of resampled residuals,
re-estimated coefficients, re-projected paths) adds coefficient
uncertainty and is the widest---by 31 October it spans the petrol gap
from $-11$ to $-40$~\cl{} and the diesel gap from $-2$ to $-47$~\cl.
Model uncertainty is deliberately kept outside the bands and shown
instead as a specification range: across oil-lag floors, fit windows
(estimating from July instead of January moves the diesel gap from
$-21.1$ to $-24.3$~\cl), balanced and regularity-filtered samples, and
Brent paths shifted by $\pm5\%$, October mean gaps lie between 15 and
19~\cl{} for petrol and 21 and 24~\cl{} for diesel, with the
distributed-lag-only diesel variant an outlier at $-26.3$
(\cref{tab:spec-range}). Under every admissible oil-containing
specification the no-cap price is materially above the realised price
throughout the window; the direction of the conclusion does not depend on
the specification, only its size does.

\section{Inflation implications}\label{sec:inflation}

ISTAT measures fuel prices for the consumer-price index (NIC) on the
1st, 11th and 21st of each month, drawing on the same MIMIT registry. We
compute month-on-month September-to-October changes of the mean reference
day price for each fuel, once as observed and once along the no-cap path,
and aggregate with explicit assumptions: the ECOICOP 07.2.2 class
``carburanti e lubrificanti'' carries a 2026 basket weight of 4.5219\%
(verified against the published weighting structure; the class includes
lubricants, a small residual we do not separately model), and we split it
evenly between petrol and diesel, reflecting their approximately equal
contributions to the class. Station observations enter with equal
weights; a regularity-filtered variant mimicking the office's preference
for stations with a consistent communication history serves as the
composition check. These are documented approximations to an aggregation
procedure the statistical office does not publish in full; we claim an
\emph{estimated contribution under stated assumptions}, not a replication
of the official calculation.

\input{paper_v2_tables}

The results are in \cref{tab:inflation}. Observed reference-day petrol
prices fell 2.3\% month-on-month where the no-cap path implies a rise of
5.3\%; diesel rose 1.6\% where the no-cap path implies 10.9\%. Weighted,
the cap's estimated contribution to October headline NIC inflation is
$-0.38$ percentage points (petrol $-0.17$, diesel $-0.21$). The
assumption sensitivities are an order of magnitude smaller than the
effect itself: 45/55 and 55/45 petrol--diesel splits give $-0.387$ and
$-0.380$; the regularity-filtered sample gives $-0.417$; and across the
specification range of \cref{sec:counterfactual} the estimate lies
between $-0.38$ and $-0.45$, with $-0.30$ under the inadmissible
autoregression-only benchmark. The dominant uncertainty is the no-cap
scenario, not the aggregation.

Two measurement notes complete the calculation. First, the
communication-time reconstruction of \cref{sec:data} matters directly for
reference-day statistics: on 1 October the extract-time mean overstated
the effective price in force by 4.2--4.9~\cl. This is a correction to
snapshot-based measurement of our reconstructed estimator; we have no
basis, and no need, to attribute error to the published index, whose
acquisition procedures may differ. Second, fuel-price components are, in
the authors' institutional experience, complete by the preliminary
release, so we do not treat the final release as a condition for these
estimates; this premise is stated as such.

\section{How the response propagated}\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{output/figures/v2_fig4_mechanism.png}
\caption{Propagation. Left: share of petrol observations below the
ceiling by brand group, 28 September--31 October. Right: the 28 October
diesel episode---mean diesel price day by day (markers shaded by the
below-ceiling share), with the old ceiling (2.261, grey) and the
mechanically adjusted ceiling (2.212, black).}
\label{fig:mech}
\end{figure}

The aggregate price decline was produced by a rapid, ordered,
geographically broad repricing, and the station panel shows its anatomy.
\Cref{fig:mech}a traces the below-ceiling share of petrol observations
by brand group: the initiator's network prices at the ceiling from the
first days; Q8 and Api-Ip converge within a week; Esso and Tamoil
slightly later; white-pump independents partially. Timing correlates
with local market structure exactly as conditional-association analysis
would have it: in duration models (\cref{tab:cox-samples}), a station ten cents
closer to the ceiling in the pre-cap week adopts sooner (log-hazard
$-0.44$ per 10~\cl{} in the universe, $-0.61$ to $-0.66$ once the leader
brands are excluded or among independents), adoption is slower the
farther the station is from its nearest competitor ($-0.10$ log-hazard
per kilometre) and from the nearest day-zero adopter ($-0.03$ to
$-0.06$ per kilometre), and larger chains adopt sooner (the global
proportional-hazards restriction is rejected, so these are
time-averaged associations rather than constant hazards; we read them as
descriptive propagation gradients, not causal effects). The
station-fixed-effects event study (\cref{tab:es-leads}) shows flat
pre-cap coefficients ($-0.002$ to $-0.004$, an order of magnitude
smaller than the day-zero jump of $+0.19$) and a plateau after four to
five days.

The ceiling became the market's focal price---not the announced nominal
level, but the level one tenth of a cent below the operational cutoff.
After the cap, 17.9\% of all petrol observations sit at exactly
1.999~\euro/l; the share of diesel observations ending in .199 rises
from 2.0\% before the cap to 11.2\% after it
(\cref{tab:endings}). When the 6 October excise step moved the diesel
ceiling to 2.261, the mass re-formed at 2.259---eight and a half per
cent of observations---rather than at the announced-style 2.19. This is
focal-point pricing of the kind documented for statutory ceilings
\parencite{zhang2020, gatsios2026}, here generated by a voluntary
commitment and matched by rivals.

Dispersion effects are best stated with numbers, because different
summaries point in different directions. The within-province
tenth-to-ninetieth percentile spread rose from about 8.1 to 14.0~\cl{}
(petrol) and 9.5 to 15.9~\cl{} (diesel) between the full pre-cap month
and the cap window---an increase of roughly seventy per cent. Standard
deviations rose for petrol (4.8 to 7.1~\cl{} within province--day) and
for diesel relative to the tighter pre-cap week, but fell for diesel
relative to the full September baseline, because the pre-cap diesel
distribution was itself unusually dispersed after the two mid-September
excise steps. The economically meaningful statistic is the gap between
adopters and non-adopters: within province--day, stations below the
ceiling priced on average 11.5 (petrol) and 11.7~\cl{} (diesel) below
stations above it, and the non-adopter tail drifted upward with oil
exactly as the counterfactual of \cref{sec:counterfactual} predicts.

The window's final days deliver a small natural experiment in
ceiling-constrained tax incidence. When the 28 October excise cut
lowered the mechanical ceiling from 2.261 to 2.212, the mean diesel price
did not move on the day (2.258 on both 27 and 28 October); by 31 October
it had fallen only 1.96~\cl{} of the 4.88~\cl{} mechanical adjustment
---a pass-through of about forty per cent in three days. The
below-ceiling share collapsed from 65.8\% to 24.0\% on the day of the
cut and recovered to 50.2\% by 31 October; not a single observation
priced at 2.211, the new bunching point, in the final three days. With
the commitment on the cap level running out, the ceiling stopped
functioning as a focal price and the network absorbed part of the tax
cut instead of passing it through---the incomplete, asymmetric
pass-through familiar from the rockets-and-feathers literature
\parencite{tappata2009, borenstein2002}, here observed at a ceiling.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A voluntary price ceiling, announced by the market leader during an oil
surge and matched by most of the network within two weeks, held Italian
pump prices far below the level implied by the pre-cap relationship
between oil and pump prices: on our baseline estimates, 17 to 21 cents
per litre below over October, with an estimated contribution to October
headline consumer-price inflation of about $-0.4$ percentage points
under transparent aggregation assumptions. The station-level panel shows
how the effect was produced: a leader-led, competition-gradient repricing
that bunched prices one tenth of a cent below the ceiling and left a
non-adopter tail drifting upward with oil, widening the price
distribution while lowering its mean.

The conclusions are conditional, and their limits are the paper's
caveats. The counterfactual assumes pre-cap pass-through would have
persisted; we validate it where possible---placebo and
expanding-origin forecasts that beat random-walk benchmarks, oil blocks
that cannot be excluded, specification ranges that bound the
answer---but a model-based no-cap path is not an experiment. The window
ends on 31 October with the commitment's hard horizon; the gradual
pass-through of the final diesel excise cut suggests the ceiling's force
was already fading. And the micro gradients we document are conditional
associations. What the episode establishes is measurement: the size of a
voluntary cap's price and inflation effects can be bounded credibly from
a complete price-communication registry, and the details of that
registry---communication times, three-decimal quoting, tax-translated
ceilings---matter for every number in it.
