\title{A Voluntary Price Cap in the Italian Retail Fuel Market: \\
\large Adoption, Counterfactual Prices, and Inflation}
\author{Working paper --- draft \today}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
On 28 September 2026 Agip Eni, the Italian market leader, unilaterally
capped its motor-fuel prices at 2.00~\euro/l (petrol) and 2.20~\euro/l
(diesel) for two months, inviting competitors to follow. We reconstruct
the daily effective price of every Italian motorway-free (\emph{stradale})
fuel station from the official price-communication registry and ask four
questions: who adopted the cap, how adoption diffused, what prices would
have been without it, and what it did to measured inflation. Within
fifteen days, 70.2\% of 39{,}423 station--fuel units priced at or below
the (fuel- and date-adjusted) cap on at least one day (any-day shares:
62.5\% petrol, 78.0\% diesel; median adoption: day~3). Cox duration
models show adoption is faster for stations with a smaller pre-cap gap to
the threshold (hazard ratio $1.4\times 10^{-2}$ per \euro/l), higher
local population density, larger chains, and smaller distances to the
nearest competitor ($-9\%$ per km) and to the nearest adopter ($-2$ to
$-5\%$ per km, stronger once the leader brands are excluded); a
station--fixed-effects event study confirms a spatial diffusion gradient
with no anticipation. The cap polarised rather than compressed the
market: province-level within-day dispersion roughly doubled in 214 of
220 province--fuel cells. To measure the consumer-price effect we build a
counterfactual: an autoregressive distributed-lag model links the
national net-of-tax price of each fuel to the Brent crude price in euro
over the pre-cap window (1 July--27 September); projected forward on the
actual oil path, it predicts an October pump price 16.3~c/l (petrol) and
24.3~c/l (diesel) above the realised one on average. On the ISTAT
reference days used for inflation measurement, month-on-month fuel-price
changes were $-2.3\%$ (petrol) and $+1.6\%$ (diesel) with the cap, against
$+4.8\%$ and $+12.1\%$ without it: the cap lowered October's headline
month-on-month inflation by roughly 0.40 percentage points. Because
daily price extracts close at 8am, a communication wave crossing a
sampling reference day additionally biases recorded averages by up to
4~c/l; communication-time reconstruction corrects it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Price caps are among the bluntest instruments of price regulation, and
their effects are notoriously hard to measure: where a cap binds, prices
cluster at the ceiling and observed dispersion collapses, confounding
changes in conduct with censoring. On 28 September 2026, Italy offered an
unusual natural experiment. Agip Eni, the market leader, announced a
\emph{voluntary} two-month cap on its retail motor-fuel prices
(2.00~\euro/l for petrol, 2.20~\euro/l for diesel, self-service), framed
as a measure against speculation. No law mandated it; competitors were
simply invited to match. Within days, the largest synchronised price-
communication wave in the market's recorded history followed: on
1 October alone, 9{,}192 stations communicated new petrol prices, led by
rival chains cutting 10--17~c/l overnight.

The announcement landed on an unusual macroeconomic backdrop. Brent
crude --- our reference for the marginal cost of motor fuels --- rose by
three quarters in euro terms over our window (an index of 100 on 1 July
reaches 173 on the cap date), so October pump prices were being pushed
upward by world markets at the very moment the cap was pushing them
down. Any statement that ``the cap lowered prices'' is therefore
incomplete without a counterfactual: the observation that prices rose
less than oil says something, but how much less, and with what
consequence for measured inflation? This paper reconstructs the whole
chain: from the diffusion of adoption across stations, to the price path
the market would have followed absent the cap, to the contribution of
that difference to official consumer-price inflation.

We ask four questions. First, \emph{who} adopted the cap: which station
and firm characteristics predict pricing at or below the threshold?
Second, \emph{how did adoption diffuse} across the network of stations:
does adoption of neighbouring stations raise a station's own adoption
hazard, as strategic-complementarity models would predict? Third,
\emph{what would prices have been without the cap}: conditional on the
oil price and the pre-cap pass-through dynamics, what net-of-tax prices
--- and hence pump prices --- would October have delivered? Fourth,
\emph{what did the cap do to measured inflation}: on the reference days
used by the national statistical institute, what month-on-month change
occurred, and what would have occurred in the counterfactual?

We answer these questions with a station-level panel reconstructed from
the MIMIT price-communication registry. The registry records every price
communication (\texttt{dtComu}) with date and time, which lets us build
the \emph{effective} daily price path of each station --- the last
communicated price as of each day --- rather than the daily snapshot
published each morning. This distinction is not academic: the daily
extracts stop at 8am, so a station communicating a lower price at 7am is
only recorded the following day. On ISTAT price-reference days (the 1st,
11th, and 21st of each month) this biases the recorded national average:
for 1 October 2026 we measure an overstatement of 3.5--4.1~c/l
(\cref{sec:data}).

Our setting has two attractive features. Because the cap was
\emph{voluntary}, adoption is a firm decision, and we can measure the
diffusion of a coordinated price cut --- not just the mechanical effect of
a legal ceiling. And because we observe the communication time, adoption
dates are exact to the day rather than contaminated by reporting lag.
For the counterfactual, the relevant margin is the \emph{net-of-tax}
price --- what the market keeps after excise and VAT are stripped out ---
because the tax calendar itself moved violently during 2026: the diesel
excise alone swung by up to 14~c/l between July and October
(\cref{sec:background}). Modelling net prices makes the counterfactual
immune to these tax interventions and lets us re-apply the actual tax
calendar to both the observed and the counterfactual path, so that tax
steps contaminate neither side of the comparison.

We find that adoption was fast but far from universal: 70.2\% of
station--fuel units priced at or below the cap at some point in the
first fifteen days (final-day shares: 52.9\% petrol, 70.3\% diesel;
median: day 3), and adoption clustered along urban, organised-firm
corridors. Hazard models (\cref{sec:survival}) show the pre-cap gap to
the threshold dominates all other covariates; distance to the nearest
adopter, population density and chain size all raise the adoption hazard
significantly. The event study (\cref{sec:panel}) shows no anticipation
and a clean diffusion gradient across day-0 adopter-distance terciles.
The cap also \emph{polarised} the market (\cref{sec:dispersion}):
within-province dispersion widened in almost every province--fuel cell,
and non-adopters became systematically more expensive relative to their
provincial market.

On the counterfactual (\cref{sec:counterfactual}), an ARDL model of the
daily national net price on lags of itself and of the Brent price in
euro --- estimated only on pre-cap data, and validated by a placebo
backtest that beats the random-walk benchmark for both fuels ---
implies October
pump prices 16.3~c/l (petrol) and 24.3~c/l (diesel) above the realised
ones on average over 28 September--31 October, and 20.4 and 26.7~c/l
above by the window's end. Translated to measurement days
(\cref{sec:inflation}): the realised month-on-month change of pump
prices averaged over the 1st, 11th and 21st was $-2.3\%$ for petrol and
$+1.6\%$ for diesel; the counterfactual delivers $+4.8\%$ and $+12.1\%$.
Using the 2026 ISTAT basket weight for motor fuels (4.52\%), the cap
lowered headline month-on-month inflation by roughly 0.40 percentage
points in October --- the difference between a $+0.38$~pp fuel
contribution and a $-0.02$~pp one.

\section{Institutional background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The market and the registry.}
Italian fuel retailers must communicate every price change to the
MIMIT (Ministry of Enterprise and Made in Italy) price-communication
registry, which publishes daily extracts by station, fuel and price
with the communication timestamp. Pump prices are liberalised; the
communicated price is the legally displayed price. Motorway
(\emph{autostradale}) and ordinary-road (\emph{stradale}) stations are
distinct markets with different cost levels; this paper restricts to
\emph{stradale} and to \emph{self-service}, the mode used by the vast
majority of purchases.

\paragraph{The voluntary cap.}
On 28 September 2026 Agip Eni --- the brand with the largest network ---
announced it would cap its self-service prices at 2.00~\euro/l (petrol)
and 2.20~\euro/l (diesel) for two months, and invited competitors to
match. The cap is a private commitment: no law mandates it, no authority
enforces it, and each rival's compliance is its own decision. Because
the announced thresholds sit close to the pre-cap national average
petrol price but clearly above the diesel excise-driven level, the two
fuels provide different margins of bite. The announced diesel cap was
framed on pre-tax-change levels: when the government removed the
remaining 5~c/l excise discount on diesel with effect from 6 October
2026, the corresponding ``compliant'' diesel threshold moved to
$2.20 + 5 \times 1.22 = 2.261$~\euro/l, the mechanical translation of a
tax change through 22\% VAT.

\paragraph{The 2026 excise calendar.}
Excise duties (accise) are set per litre and enter the pump price
additively; VAT of 22\% applies multiplicatively to the sum of net price
and excise, so $P^{gross} = (P^{net} + \tau)\times 1.22$. In 2026 the
government adjusted excises repeatedly. Within our window: petrol excise
rose from 62.29 to 67.29~c/l on 4 July; diesel followed the same step on
4 July, was cut by 14~c/l on 28 July (to 53.29~c/l), then raised to
57.29 (18 September), 62.29 (26 September), 67.29 (6 October, remaining
discount removed by decree-law 162/2026) and cut again to 63.29~c/l on
28 October. These are movements of the same order as --- sometimes larger
than --- the daily dynamics the cap was meant to control, and a pump-price
series is uninterpretable without them. All counterfactual comparisons
in this paper therefore work with net prices, and re-apply the actual
excise calendar identically to both sides.

\paragraph{How official statistics measure fuel prices.}
ISTAT collects fuel prices for the consumer price index (NIC) on the
1st, 11th and 21st of each month, the three \emph{reference days}
whose monthly average enters the index. Motor fuels (ECOICOP class
07.2.2, ``Carburanti e lubrificanti'') carry 4.5219\% of the 2026 NIC
basket (definitive weights). Two features matter for us. First, the
measured month-on-month change is the difference between the average of
the three October reference days and that of the three September days,
so a policy that bites mid-month is captured with a lag and a blend ---
here, the cap starts nine days before the first October reference day.
Second, the collection is timed on the day's registry state, which ---
because extracts close at 8am --- can mis-date a communication wave
crossing a reference day (\cref{sec:dtcomu}).

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Price ceilings as focal points.}
Theory predicts that a binding ceiling can coordinate oligopolists on the
administered price when demand is inelastic. The best empirical evidence
comes from China's administered gasoline ceilings, which act as focal
points producing price uniformity \parencite{zhang2020}. Legislated
gasoline price ceilings have been studied in Canada
\parencite{sen2011}, Greece \parencite{gatsios2026} and Colombia
\parencite{quiguanas2025}, generally finding modest price effects and
concerns about capture; Spain's transition from ceiling to liberalised
pricing is documented by \textcite{contin1999}. Closest to our
counterfactual exercise, \textcite{carranza2015} study price controls in
gasoline retail and show their effects depend on market structure. Our
setting differs decisively: the cap is \emph{voluntary} and unenforced,
so adoption is a firm choice. We observe the diffusion path itself, and
we find uniformity is partial --- a persistent non-adopter tail coexists
with a mass of followers, in line with the dispersion persistence found
in regulated Colombian retailing \parencite{quiguanas2025}.

\paragraph{Crude-oil pass-through.}
A large literature documents asymmetric pass-through --- ``rockets and
feathers'' --- from crude to retail gasoline
\parencite{borenstein1997, tappata2009}, driven by consumer search
\parencite{lewis2011} and stronger where local market power is high
\parencite{deltas2008, verlinda2008}; see also \textcite{bettendorf2003}
for the Dutch market and \textcite{atil2013} for nonlinearities.
\textcite{albulescu2021} document strong fuel-price co-movement across
France, Germany and Italy, consistent with a common crude-oil driver.
This literature establishes both that retail fuel prices track crude
with distributed lags --- the identifying assumption behind our
counterfactual --- and that pass-through is state-dependent, which is
why the lag structure is selected by an information criterion (BIC)
rather than imposed coefficient by coefficient.

\paragraph{Retail fuel price dynamics.}
Retail fuel prices are strategic complements with locally heterogeneous
adjustment --- the mechanism our hazard estimates quantify. Sticky rather
than cycling pricing is the norm in several markets
\parencite{noel2007a,noel2007b,dehaas2024}, consistent with what we
observe at the cap.

\paragraph{Consumer search and price dispersion.}
Equilibrium dispersion arises even with identical agents
\parencite{reinganum1979, stiglitz1982}; gasoline applications include
\textcite{chandra2011} and \textcite{noel2018}. \textcite{
pennerstorfer2020} show dispersion is non-monotone in information. Our
bimodal post-cap distribution --- a mass at the cap plus a non-adopter
tail --- is a stark departure from these smooth-dispersion benchmarks.

\paragraph{Strategic complementarities and leadership.}
Sequential ``domino'' pricing is documented in gasoline markets by
\textcite{atkinson2008}. Second-mover advantage is generic in Bertrand
settings \parencite{amir2007}; direct estimates of price-setting
complementarities are provided by \textcite{amiti2016} and, in housing,
\textcite{guren2018} --- momentum arising from complementarities is
precisely the adoption pattern we measure. Network oligopoly structure
shapes markups \parencite{pellegrino2025} and vertical integration
affects rivals' costs \parencite{hastings2005, borenstein2002}.

\paragraph{The Italian fuel market.}
Price-transparency shocks on Italian motorways cut nearby prices by about
3\% \parencite{rossi2015}; spatial interaction and territorial factors
shape Italian retail fuel pricing \parencite{bergantino2020,
alderighi2015}; and non-adoption of price cuts has distributional stakes
\parencite{mattioli2023}. Price-cap incentive theory originates in the
utility-regulation literature \parencite{braeutigam1993, cabral1991}.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
We provide the first station-level, communication-based measurement of a
\emph{voluntary} cap's diffusion, showing that a unilateral cap triggers
competitor matching along a spatial gradient; we quantify the roles of
pre-cap price position, brand, chain size and local spillovers in the
adoption decision; we build a tax-immune oil-based counterfactual that
quantifies the cap's consumer-price effect over a window in which crude
rose by three quarters; and we document a reference-day sampling bias of
3.5--4.1~c/l for official statistics when a communication wave crosses a
measurement date, together with the cap's contribution to headline
month-on-month inflation.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources}

We combine six datasets:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MIMIT price communications} (daily extracts in our MongoDB
cache): every price communication by every Italian fuel station, with
station identifier, fuel, price, and communication timestamp
(\texttt{dtComu}).
\item \textbf{MIMIT station registry} (\emph{anagrafica}): Gestore
(operator), Bandiera (brand), type, municipality and coordinates.
\item \textbf{ISTAT 2021 census population grid}: resident population in
$1\,\mathrm{km}^2$ cells (EPSG:3035).
\item \textbf{ISTAT administrative boundaries} (2026): province and
region polygons.
\item \textbf{Brent crude and USD/EUR exchange rate}: daily spot Brent
(US\$/bbl, EIA series via FRED \texttt{DCOILBRENTEU}) and the ECB euro
reference rate (US\$ per \euro, via FRED \texttt{DEXUSEU}). We convert
to euro per barrel ($B^{\euro} = B^{\$} / e_{\$/\euro}$) and
forward-fill weekends and TARGET holidays onto the daily fuel-price
calendar (both series publish on all 88 business days of the window;
the conversion moves the euro appreciation in our favour by only about
2\% over four months).
\item \textbf{Excise calendar}: statutory rates per fuel with
application dates, from the official decrees recorded in our tax cache
(\cref{sec:background}); VAT at 22\% throughout.
\end{itemize}
Our window runs from 1 July to 31 October 2026 and covers
\emph{stradale} stations only (motorway stations are a separate market).
The adoption analyses of \cref{sec:survival,sec:panel} use
communications through 12 October (censoring date, see below); the
price, counterfactual and inflation analyses use effective prices
through 31 October. The station registry covers 39{,}423 stradale
station--fuel units (19{,}746 petrol, 19{,}677 diesel); the daily
effective-price panel averages about 19{,}800 petrol and 19{,}700
diesel stations per day, 98\% of which report essentially every day
(the balanced-sample robustness of \cref{sec:counterfactual} uses this
98\%).

\subsection{From communications to effective prices}
\label{sec:dtcomu}

Daily extracts snapshot the market before each day's communication wave
(extracts close at 8am; the bulk of communications arrives between 6:20
and 8:00). A station's ``price on day $D$'' read off the extract may
therefore reflect a communication made days earlier. We instead define
the effective price on day $D$ as the last communication with
$\texttt{dtComu} \le D$, propagated forward. A communication made on day
$D$ prices day $D$.

Two consequences. First, adoption dates are exact: a station counts as
adopted on the first post-cap day its effective price is at or below the
cap. Second, we can measure the extract's \emph{snapshot bias}. On
1 October 2026 --- an ISTAT reference day --- zero same-day
communications appear in the day's extract: the recorded national average
overstates the true day-1 average by 3.47~c/l for petrol and 4.06~c/l for
diesel (9{,}192 and 9{,}221 stations communicated that day, nearly all
after the cutoff; on the stradale estimation sample used in
\cref{sec:inflation} the same bias is 4.2 and 4.9~c/l). Official
statistics reading prices off extracts on reference days are subject to
this bias whenever a wave crosses a sampling date.

\subsection{From pump prices to net prices}
\label{sec:netprices}

The pump price decomposes as $P^{gross} = (P^{net} + \tau)\times(1 +
\text{VAT})$, so the net price is $P^{net} = P^{gross}/1.22 -
\tau(\text{fuel}, t)$, with the excise $\tau$ following the statutory
calendar of \cref{sec:background}. Net prices are the object of the
counterfactual analysis: they remove every excise and VAT intervention
by construction, isolating what the market sets. Around excise steps the
net price is noisy for a few days --- stations pass the tax change at
different speeds, so the net price temporarily absorbs margin ---
transitory blips that the net-price regression of
\cref{sec:counterfactual} simply leaves in the error, since a
tax-stripped target should not respond to tax dates.

\subsection{Variables}
\label{sec:variables}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Adoption}: effective price $\le$ cap (2.00/2.20~\euro/l) on
day $t \ge$ 2026-09-28. Time to adoption $T_i$ measured in days from
28 September; stations never adopting are censored at 12 October.
\item \emph{Distance to nearest station} (\texttt{nn\_dist\_km}) and
\emph{nearest at-cap station} (\texttt{cap\_dist\_km}): great-circle
distances in EPSG:3035 computed daily over all priced stations of the
same fuel (KD-tree).
\item \emph{Local population}: 2021 census population of the station's
containing 1-km cell (\texttt{pop\_density\_cell}).
\item \emph{Chain size}: number of stations nationwide under the same
Gestore (\texttt{n\_stations\_gestore}).
\item \emph{Pre-cap gap}: mean effective price 21--27 September minus
the fuel-specific cap (\texttt{pre\_gap\_vs\_cap}).
\item \emph{National daily price series}: the cross-sectional mean of
effective prices per fuel and day (self-service, stradale), separately
in gross and net terms; the basis for \cref{sec:counterfactual}
and \cref{sec:inflation}.
\item \emph{Brand} (\texttt{bandiera}) and \emph{region} fixed effects.
\end{itemize}
Coordinates are harmonised to WGS84; 99.8\% of stations are assigned to
a province (2 broken-coordinate stations dropped; 53 unassigned
stations kept with missing province).

\section{Descriptive evidence}
\label{sec:descriptive}

Adoption was fast and front-loaded. \Cref{fig:rawshares} plots the exact
daily share of station--fuel units priced at or below the cap, one
panel per fuel. On the cap day itself 21\% priced at or below the
threshold; the share then rose to 51\% by day 4. The petrol and diesel
paths track each other almost one-for-one through 5 October (54.0\% and
55.1\%). On 6 October the government removed the remaining 5~c/l
excise discount on diesel, and we raise the diesel cap threshold
accordingly (by $5 \times 1.22 = 6.1$~c/l including VAT; the dotted
line in the lower panel). The diesel share jumps 11 percentage points
overnight, from 55.1\% to 66.3\%, while petrol is unchanged --- this
discontinuity is the tax adjustment at work, not diffusion.

The jump reflects genuine repricing rather than a communication or
coding artefact. Between 5 and 6 October, 42\% of tracked diesel
stations raised their price by 4--8~c/l (mean change $+2.3$~c/l), and
66\% of the stations priced at or below 2.20~\euro/l on 5 October
reappeared in the (2.20, 2.261]~\euro/l band the next day. Of the
diesel units counted compliant on 6 October in that band, 90\% carry
a price communication dated 6 October itself (only 10\% are carried
forward from earlier dtComu), so operators actively re-communicated
prices just under the new threshold rather than the threshold move
mechanically reclassifying stale prices. By 12 October, 62.5\% of petrol
and 78.0\% of diesel units price at or below the (fuel- and
date-adjusted) cap.

The overall map (\cref{fig:mapall}) shows a clear geography: adoption
was highest around Rome, along the Tyrrhenian corridor and in parts of
the North-West (up to 69\% of units), and lowest in Alpine provinces
and parts of the South and the islands (down to 31\%). The range is
wide --- a 38-percentage-point gap between the extremes --- and the
north--south gradient is not monotone: some southern provinces
(Abruzzo's corridor, coastal Puglia) match northern adoption levels.
The independents' map is strikingly different (\cref{fig:mappb}):
independent adoption rates are low almost everywhere (national mean
23\%, and exactly zero in the darkest provinces) with a sharp pocket of
convergence along the central Adriatic axis --- from Umbria through the
Marche -- Abruzzo corridor --- where independents followed the leader's
network, and near-zero adoption in the Alpine arc and the islands.
Independents responded to local pressure, not to the national
announcement.

\section{Who adopts? Survival analysis}
\label{sec:survival}

We estimate Cox proportional-hazards models \parencite{cox1972regression}
of time to adoption. Duration is days from 28 September to first at-cap
day; never-adopters are right-censored at 12 October, and the diesel
threshold is tax-adjusted from 6 October. Because brand
differences are so stark in the raw data --- and because the leader's own
stations adopted almost mechanically by construction --- we estimate the
model on three samples: the \emph{universe} of station--fuel units; the
market \emph{excluding} Agip Eni and Q8 (the two brands that announced
at-cap prices in the first wave); and \emph{Pompe Bianche only}
(independent stations). Standard errors are clustered by Gestore
(operator). Chain size counts stations nationwide under the same Gestore.

\input{paper_tables}

\Cref{tab:cox-samples} reports coefficients (hazard ratios are
$e^{\hat\beta}$). Three results are robust across samples. First, the
\textbf{pre-cap gap dominates}: its coefficient grows (in magnitude) from
$-4.2$ in the universe to $-6.5$ excluding the leader brands and
$-5.4$ for independents --- stations already pricing near the cap adopted
almost mechanically, and this remains the single strongest predictor
once leader stations are removed. Second, \textbf{spatial spillovers
strengthen when the leader brands are removed}: the nearest-adopter
distance coefficient roughly doubles from $-0.025$ (universe) to
$-0.052$ (excl.\ Agip Eni/Q8) and $-0.062$ (Pompe Bianche). This is exactly the
pattern strategic-complementarity models predict: leader-brand rows
absorbed the spatial variation, and once they are excluded, the
neighbourhood diffusion effect emerges clearly. Independents respond to
what nearby stations do, not to the cap announcement itself. Third,
\textbf{chain size matters most for independents} ($0.0016$ versus
$0.0001$): small local chains (gestori) coordinated their response,
while brand-level headquarters decided for the organised brands.

Robustness. A Weibull AFT model gives the same signs and significance
per sample; gestore-clustered standard errors leave all conclusions
unchanged; the PH assumption is examined in \cref{app:diag} and the
reported coefficients should be read as window-averaged effects.

\section{Market dynamics: panel event study and adoption probabilities}
\label{sec:panel}

\subsection{Event study}

We estimate station fixed-effects models of the daily at-cap indicator
over the event window $[-7, +7]$ around the cap:
\begin{equation}
\text{at\_cap}_{it} = \sum_{k \ne -1} \beta_k \,
\mathbf{1}[t - T_0 = k] + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:es}
\end{equation}
with day $-1$ as reference and gestore-clustered errors. Because
\cref{fig:rawshares} already shows the raw adoption path --- the data are
exhaustive, so the shares themselves carry no sampling uncertainty ---
the event study serves only as a robustness check that the pattern is
not an artefact of composition. Its coefficients are reported in
\cref{app:es} and mirror \cref{fig:rawshares}: no pre-cap movement, a
jump at day 0, and a plateau from day 5.

To trace diffusion, we split station--fuel units into terciles by their
distance to the nearest station that adopted on day 0 (cut points:
1.08 and 3.15~km) and plot the exact daily at-cap share of each tercile
in \cref{fig:capdist}. The three curves never cross. At every point in
time, stations near a day-0 adopter adopt earlier and more often: on
5 October, 62.7\% of the near tercile is at the cap versus 44.3\% of
the far tercile --- an 18-percentage-point gradient --- and the far
tercile never catches up over our window. Note that the far tercile's
curve is not flat either: it rises throughout, so diffusion eventually
reached remote stations too, but with a lag of several days. This
ordering is exactly what local strategic complementarities predict, and
it is invisible in the aggregate series of \cref{fig:rawshares}.

\subsection{Who adopts? Adoption probabilities}
\label{sec:lpm}

The survival analysis of \cref{sec:survival} conditions on time;
here we ask the complementary question: given the full covariate set of
\cref{sec:variables}, \emph{which} stations ended up adopting at all?
We estimate linear probability models (OLS with gestore-clustered
standard errors)
\begin{equation}
\text{adopted}_i = \mathbf{x}_i'\gamma + \mu_{b(i)} + \delta_{r(i)}
+ u_i,
\label{eq:lpm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_i$ collects the continuous and fuel covariates,
$\mu_b$ are brand fixed effects (within-sample \(\ge\)100-observation
rule) and $\delta_r$ region fixed effects. \Cref{tab:lpm-core} reports
the core coefficients for the same three samples as
\cref{tab:cox-samples}; the full brand and region coefficients appear in
\cref{app:lpm-s1,app:lpm-s2}.

\input{paper_tables_s6}

The LPM confirms and sharpens the survival results. The pre-cap gap is
by far the strongest predictor in every sample: $-1.31$ probability
points per cent/litre of pre-cap excess in the universe sample, $-1.56$
excluding the leader brands, $-1.05$ for independents. Distance to the
nearest day-0 adopter is negative and significant in all three samples
($-0.010$ universe, $-0.014$ excluding the leader brands, $-0.010$ for
Pompe Bianche), while the distance to the nearest station
(competitor) is smaller and only marginally significant for
independents ($-0.018$, $p = 0.009$): proximity to \emph{adopters}
predicts adoption at least as strongly as proximity to competitors,
and for independents the two effects are of similar magnitude once
measurement noise is accounted for. Chain size is positive and
significant in the universe and Pompe Bianche samples (insignificant
once the leader brands are removed, whose networks coordinate centrally)
and roughly seventeen times larger for Pompe Bianche, echoing the
gestore-coordination reading of \cref{sec:survival}. Population density
is economically negligible once brand and region are controlled. Fit is
moderate (adjusted $R^2 = 0.25$--$0.34$), as expected for individual
adoption decisions; diagnostics are in \cref{app:diag}.

\section{Dispersion and competitiveness}
\label{sec:dispersion}

\paragraph{Province-level dispersion.}
We compute, for each province $\times$ fuel $\times$ phase (pre:
21--27 September; post: 28 September -- 12 October), the daily
within-province standard deviation of effective prices, and compare
phases. The SD rose in \textbf{214 of the 220 province--fuel cells}
(97.3\%): on average from 51 to 87~c/l for petrol and from 65 to
90~c/l for diesel --- a doubling. \Cref{fig:sdpre,fig:sdpost} show the
distribution of the shift. Throughout the paper, maps use a blue--red
scale: \emph{blue marks low values and red marks high values}. The
province-level standard deviation rises from a median of 50~c/l
pre-cap to 90~c/l post-cap, and the whole distribution shifts right
(\cref{fig:sdcapdist}, top panel): 214 of the 220 province--fuel cells
see their SD increase. \Cref{fig:sdpre,fig:sdpost} in \cref{app:maps}
show the geography: pre-cap the map is predominantly light blue
(typical SDs 28--150~c/l); post-cap red spreads along the Alps and the
Apennines --- the provinces where dispersion widened most are those
where the adopter share was intermediate, mixing cap-priced with
above-cap stations. Provincial SDs after the cap run 71--235~c/l, and
no province returns to its pre-cap level. The cap did not compress the
distribution --- it split it. A mass of adopters sits exactly at the
threshold while a non-adopter tail holds prices above it, producing a
bimodal distribution unlike the smooth equilibrium-dispersion
benchmarks \parencite{reinganum1979, stiglitz1982}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{output/figures/fig_dispersion_hist.png}
\caption{Province-level distributions before (blue) and after (red) the
cap. Top: within-province standard deviation of effective prices.
Bottom: non-adopters' mean gap to the provincial daily mean.}

The consumer reading is correspondingly uneven: where adoption was high,
prices fell to the cap for most of the market; where the non-adopter
tail dominated, prices barely moved. Given that adoption tracked
population density and chain presence, the gains concentrate in urban,
organised-firm corridors --- with distributional implications for
car-dependent rural areas \parencite{mattioli2023}.

\section{Counterfactual prices: what would the market have done?}
\label{sec:counterfactual}

The adoption evidence says who followed the leader; it does not say what
the cap was worth. Over our window Brent crude in euro rose from an
index of 100 (1 July) to 173 on the cap date and 177 at the end of
October --- a supply-side impulse that, absent the cap, would have pushed
October pump prices up. Measuring the cap therefore requires a
counterfactual net-price path: what the market's net margins would have
been had the pre-cap relationship with oil continued.

\subsection{Net prices tracked oil --- until the cap}
\label{sec:indexfig}

\Cref{fig:index} normalises the three relevant series --- Brent in euro
per barrel, the national net price of petrol and the national net price
of diesel --- to 100 on 1 July 2026, the first day of our window. Two
facts stand out. First, before the cap the three series move together:
over 1 July--27 September, Brent rises 67\% and the two net prices rise
28\% and 44\% --- co-movement, with the net prices responding less than
proportionally and diesel more than petrol, consistent with partial and
lagged pass-through \parencite{borenstein1997, tappata2009}. Second,
from 28 September the net prices stop following oil: petrol's index
falls from 125 to 114 and diesel's from 140 to 130 by 31 October, while
Brent's climbs to 177. The visual break is the cap at work on the
market's margin.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{output/figures/fig_brent_net_index.png}
\caption{Brent crude (\euro/bbl) and national net prices (petrol,
diesel), all indexed to 100 on 1 July 2026. The red dotted line marks
the voluntary cap (28 September). Before the cap the three series
co-move; after it, the net prices decouple from oil.}
\label{fig:index}
\end{figure}

\subsection{An ARDL model of net prices}
\label{sec:ardl}

We model the daily national net price of each fuel as an
autoregressive distributed-lag (ARDL) process in logs,
\begin{equation}
\ln P^{net}_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i \ln P^{net}_{t-i}
+ \sum_{j=0}^{q} \theta_j \ln B^{\euro}_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t,
\label{eq:ardl}
\end{equation}
where $B^{\euro}$ is Brent in \euro/bbl. Because the dependent
variable is net of excise and VAT by construction, the tax calendar is
already stripped from the target and no tax term appears in the
equation: statutory tax changes should not move a net price, and the
transitory blips they do leave in the measured series --- stations pass
a tax change at different speeds --- stay in the error, where they
belong. The ARDL form
\parencite{pesaran2001} lets the data pick both the speed of
pass-through and the long-run level relationship; we search
$p \in [0,10]$, $q \in [7,10]$ by BIC on the pre-cap window, 1 July--27
September (82 daily observations after lags for each fuel): petrol
selects $(4,7)$, diesel $(1,7)$. The seven-lag floor requires at least
a week of Brent dynamics in the polynomial --- the horizon over which
wholesale-to-retail pass-through typically completes
\parencite{borenstein1997, tappata2009} --- and disciplines the
autoregressive root on this short sample; \cref{tab:ardl-robust} shows
the results are insensitive to it. The grid search is exhaustive and
reproduces the \textsf{ARDL} package's unconstrained selections when
the floor is lifted (\cref{app:diag}).

\Cref{tab:ardl} reports the specification and fit. The models track the
net prices closely ($R^2 = 0.999$ petrol, $0.995$ diesel), and the
implied \emph{long-run pass-through elasticity} of net prices to Brent
in euro --- $\sum_j \theta_j / (1 - \sum_i \phi_i)$ --- is $0.43$ for
petrol and $0.50$ for diesel: a 10\% rise in the euro oil price raises
the net price by 4.3--5.0\% once lags play out. The adjustment half-life
is about 26 days for petrol and 8 days for diesel. Diagnostics
(\cref{app:diag}: Breusch--Godfrey, RESET, Newey--West standard errors)
are discussed there; in brief, petrol's Breusch--Godfrey statistic rejects at short lags
(read inference with the reported Newey--West errors) while its RESET
passes, and diesel's diagnostics are clean --- its RESET now passes at
the 5\% level ($p = 0.08$), the deeper oil polynomial absorbing the
dynamics that the most parsimonious specification left in the error.

\input{paper_tables_cf}

\paragraph{Placebo backtest.}
Before trusting a projection, we check that the design can forecast a
period it was not fitted on. Refitting to 13 September and projecting
14--27 September --- still pre-cap, so the truth is observable --- the
recursive projection attains a root-mean-square error of 1.93~c/l
(petrol) and 4.01~c/l (diesel) on the net price, against 3.69 and
5.07~c/l for a no-change random walk, with biases of $+1.9$ and
$-2.9$~c/l. The ARDL roughly halves the benchmark error for petrol and
cuts it by a fifth for diesel --- whose remaining shortfall is
concentrated on the two excise steps inside the backtest window (18 and
26 September; excluding those transition days the RMSE falls to
3.10~c/l). The bias signs also bound the counterfactual: petrol's
device under-predicts the net price, making that gap conservative,
while diesel's over-prediction would, if it carried into October, shave
roughly 3~c/l off a 24~c/l gap.

\subsection{The counterfactual pump price}
\label{sec:cfresults}

From 28 September we project \cref{eq:ardl} recursively: the model's
own projected net prices feed its lags, while Brent follows the actual
path --- actual post-cap net prices are never used, since the cap
contaminated them. The counterfactual pump price is then
$\widehat{P}^{gross}_t = (\widehat{P}^{net}_t + \tau_t) \times 1.22$,
with the \emph{actual} excise calendar $\tau_t$: both the observed and
the counterfactual market face the same taxes, so the comparison
isolates the cap, not tax policy. Uncertainty bands come from a
residual bootstrap (2{,}000 draws, 95\% intervals).

\Cref{fig:cf} shows the result. The counterfactual petrol price starts
where the market started (2.16 vs 2.13~\euro/l on 28 September, a gap
of 3.2~c/l) and climbs with oil while the actual price falls to the
cap: by 31 October the gap is 20.4~c/l. Diesel's counterfactual climbs
from 2.38 to 2.51~\euro/l --- a 26.7~c/l gap at the end --- while the
actual price stays pinned near the (tax-adjusted) cap. Averaged over
the 34 post-cap days, the cap saved consumers 16.3~c/l per litre of
petrol and 24.3~c/l per litre of diesel relative to the oil-following
counterfactual. The bands widen slowly ($\pm$3.6~c/l for
petrol and $\pm$5.9~c/l for diesel at the 34-day horizon), so the gap
remains a multiple of its uncertainty from mid-October on.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
Four checks. First, the seven-lag floor is not doing the work:
relaxing it to five, two or none moves petrol's selected order only
between $(4,7)$ and $(5,0)$, with long-run elasticities 0.43--0.46 and
mean gaps $-16.2$ to $-16.5$~c/l, while the diesel gap moves within
$-24.3$ to $-27.5$~c/l --- and relaxing the floor \emph{degrades}
diesel's backtest, from 4.01~c/l with seven oil lags to 5.03 with
none, so the floor is a strength rather than a constraint. Collapsing
to a pure distributed lag on Brent (no autoregressive term) leaves
petrol essentially unchanged while degrading diesel's, whose margins
adjust too persistently for a static lag polynomial. Oil is
indispensable in every specification: HAC block-exclusion of the Brent
terms rejects massively in both fuels ($p < 10^{-4}$), whereas an
autoregression with \emph{no} oil term --- economically untenable ---
fits almost as well in sample yet forecasts worse and understates the
effect (\cref{tab:ardl-robust}). Second,
restricting the sample to the 98\% of
stations that report essentially every day (a balanced panel) moves the
national daily means by fractions of a cent --- composition is not
driving the gap. Third, using the naive 8am-cut extract averages
instead of effective prices would add the snapshot bias of
\cref{sec:dtcomu} to the actual series on reference days (4--5~c/l
\emph{against} the cap), making the counterfactual gap larger, not
smaller. Fourth, the result is not an artefact of the euro: the
USD/\euro{} rate moved less than 2\% over the window, and converting
Brent at any pre-window average rate changes the October gaps by well
under 1~c/l. The main caveat is sample length: 85--88 daily
observations support a parsimonious ARDL but not a full cointegration
analysis, and the projection asks the pre-cap dynamic to persist for
34 days --- the backtest suggests this is a reasonable, not a
costless, assumption.

\section{From pump prices to measured inflation}
\label{sec:inflation}

ISTAT measures fuel prices for the NIC on the 1st, 11th and 21st of
each month; the monthly fuel index moves with the average of the three
reference days, and motor fuels carry 4.5219\% of the 2026 basket
(definitive weights). \Cref{tab:inflation} compares the realised
September-to-October month-on-month change of the national average pump
price --- averaged over the reference days, exactly the object the
index tracks --- with the counterfactual of \cref{sec:counterfactual}
on the same days. Pre-cap reference days (September) have no
counterfactual by construction: there the no-cap path \emph{is} the
observed path.

\input{paper_tables_infl}

The result is stark. With the cap, the October reference-day average
was 2.042~\euro/l for petrol and 2.258~\euro/l for diesel,
month-on-month changes of $-2.34\%$ and $+1.58\%$: petrol \emph{fell}
into an oil-price surge. Without the cap, the counterfactual implies
2.192 and 2.492~\euro/l --- $+4.85\%$ and $+12.13\%$ month-on-month.
The cap therefore changed the fuels' month-on-month rate by $-7.18$
percentage points (petrol) and $-10.55$ (diesel), or $-8.86$~pp for
motor fuels combined. Note that both series face the same tax calendar:
the diesel excise steps of 6 October fall on the actual and the
counterfactual alike, so the difference is conduct, not fiscal policy.

Weighted by the NIC basket, motor fuels contributed $-0.02$~pp to
headline month-on-month October inflation with the cap, against
$+0.38$~pp in the counterfactual: the voluntary cap lowered October's
measured month-on-month inflation by roughly $\mathbf{0.40}$
percentage points. For a measure framed against ``speculation''
during an oil surge, this is the cleanest statement of what it bought:
the market's own margin compression absorbed the entire
counterfactual fuel contribution rather than passing it to pumps.

A measurement caveat ties back to \cref{sec:dtcomu}: the
\emph{recorded} (8am-cut) averages on 1 October overstate the effective
ones by 4.2~c/l (petrol) and 4.9~c/l (diesel) on our sample, because
the adoption wave communicated after 8am was mis-dated. Official
October fuel inflation thus mixes the cap effect with a data artifact
in the first half of the month; effective-price reconstruction, as
used here, is the robust measurement.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What did the cap buy, and at whose cost?}
Our counterfactual prices the cap's consumer benefit at 16.3~c/l
(petrol) and 24.3~c/l (diesel) per litre over the first post-cap month
--- 0.40~pp of October headline inflation. Unlike an excise cut, which
lowers prices for everyone at direct fiscal cost, the voluntary cap
operates on margins: the leader gave up its own premium and invited
rivals to compress theirs. The cost side is invisible in our data ---
who absorbed the cut (refining margins, retail margins, or the leader's
brand premium) is a question for company accounts --- but the
measurement is not: the benefit accrues wherever adoption happened.

\paragraph{The cap and the tax lever interact.}
The 2026 excise calendar moved seven times, with steps up to 14~c/l ---
the same instrument the cap was defending consumers with. On 6 October
the diesel cap itself rose by 6.1~c/l because the state removed a
discount; \cref{fig:rawshares} shows the compliance share jumping 11~pp
overnight as operators repriced just under the new threshold. An
anti-inflation commitment on net prices would be immune to this
interference; a cap defined on gross prices is hostage to every tax
move.

\paragraph{A voluntary cap polarises.}
The adoption analysis (\cref{sec:dispersion}) and the price levels
(\cref{fig:cf}) tell one story from two angles: the cap compressed the
follower mass onto a ceiling and let a non-adopter tail drift upward
with oil --- province-level dispersion doubled, and non-adopters ended
October 20--31~c/l above where a cap-follower stood. The consumer
benefit of a voluntary cap is thus rationed by geography and brand, an
equity wrinkle for a policy advertised as protecting all drivers
\parencite{mattioli2023}.

\paragraph{Sunset effects.}
The cap was announced for two months. Our window ends on 31 October,
roughly a third of the way into that horizon; strategic behaviour
around expiry --- front-loading purchases, or a coordinated exit ---
is outside these data and is the natural next measurement.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A voluntary, temporary price cap announced by the Italian market leader
achieved partial market-wide matching within days --- by 12 October
62.5\% of petrol and 78.0\% of diesel station--fuel units priced at or
below the (tax-adjusted) threshold. Adoption was primarily a firm
decision --- but not only: once the leader brands are excluded, local
spillovers emerge as a first-order driver, with the adoption hazard
falling 7--14\% per kilometre from the nearest adopter and independent
stations following in dense, organised corridors while ignoring the cap
elsewhere. Pre-cap price position dominates everywhere: stations
already pricing near the threshold adopted almost mechanically. The cap
polarised rather than compressed the market: dispersion widened in
almost every province, and non-adopters became relatively more
expensive.

The price counterfactual turns these adoption facts into consumer
value. An ARDL model of net prices on Brent-in-euro --- estimated on
pre-cap data only, and validated by a placebo backtest that cuts the
random-walk forecast error for both fuels --- implies that without
the
cap, October pump prices would have averaged 16.3~c/l (petrol) and
24.3~c/l (diesel) higher, with the gap reaching 20 and 27~c/l by month
end. On ISTAT reference days the cap changed the month-on-month fuel
inflation rate by $-7.2$~pp (petrol) and $-10.5$~pp (diesel): realised
October fuel-price inflation of $-2.3\%$ and $+1.6\%$ would have been
$+4.8\%$ and $+12.1\%$ in the oil-following counterfactual. In headline
terms, the cap lowered October's month-on-month NIC inflation by
roughly 0.40 percentage points.

Three caveats. First, the adoption window is fifteen post-cap days and
the counterfactual extends to thirty-four; the two-month cap horizon
may produce further convergence or reversal around expiry --- strategic
pricing near sunset dates is unobservable here. Second, adoption is
measured from communications; enforcement (actual pump prices versus
communicated ones) is outside our data. Third, the counterfactual
assumes the pre-cap oil pass-through dynamic would have persisted; it
survives a placebo backtest, alternative lag selections, a balanced
panel, and any plausible exchange-rate counterfactual, but it cannot
rule out that non-cap conduct would itself have changed after 28
September. Coordinates and brand classifications inherit MIMIT data
quality.

For measurement, our snapshot-bias result is a caution for official
statistics: reference-day sampling from 8am-cut extracts mis-dated an
entire communication wave in October 2026, overstating reference-day
averages by 3.5--4.1~c/l. Communication-time reconstruction, as
implemented here, is the robust alternative.

\section{Model diagnostics}
\label{app:diag}

This appendix reports diagnostics for the Cox models of
\cref{tab:cox-samples}, the LPMs of \cref{tab:lpm-core}, and the ARDL
counterfactual models of \cref{tab:ardl}.

\paragraph{Collinearity.} The continuous covariates are close to
orthogonal: all pairwise correlations are below 0.62, the largest VIF is
1.6 (nearest-station distance), and the condition number of the
covariate correlation matrix is 4.5--5.7 across samples --- far below
conventional concern thresholds (VIF $> 10$, condition number $> 30$).
Pre-cap gap and the distance measures thus carry independent variation;
coefficients are not fragile to the inclusion of the others.

\paragraph{Proportional hazards (Cox).} Scaled Schoenfeld-residual
tests (\texttt{cox.zph}, KM transform) reject global PH in all samples,
driven by \emph{chain size} and, in the universe sample, the
\emph{pre-cap gap} (their coefficients attenuate as weaker,
further-from-cap stations adopt over the window). The qualitative
ranking of covariates is unaffected; the reported coefficients should
be read as window-averaged effects.

\paragraph{Functional form (Cox).} Martingale-residual correlations
with each continuous covariate are $\le |0.08|$ in all samples ---
no evidence of misspecified linearity for the distance, density and
gap variables at this scale.

\paragraph{LPM specification.} Pearson residuals have mean zero by
construction and are calibrated in the aggregate (mean predicted
adoption equals the observed share to four decimals in every sample).
The Ramsey RESET rejects exact linearity in the universe and
excl.-Agip/Q8 samples ($p < 0.001$), which is expected: the true
response surface is a probability bounded in $[0,1]$, and the LPM is
used for its readable coefficients, not for out-of-range extrapolation.
Heteroskedasticity inherent to binary outcomes is handled by the
gestore-clustered (Huber--White) standard errors reported throughout.

\paragraph{ARDL diagnostics.} Petrol ARDL$(4,7)$: $R^2 = 0.999$,
residual SD 0.24\% of the net price; RESET (powers 2--3) does not
reject ($p = 0.29$); Breusch--Godfrey rejects at orders 7 and 14
($p = 0.001$ and $0.013$), so coefficient inference uses Newey--West
(lag 7) standard errors. Diesel
ARDL$(1,7)$: $R^2 = 0.995$, residual SD 0.68\%; Breusch--Godfrey is
clean ($p = 0.26$ and $0.39$); RESET passes at the 5\% level
($p = 0.08$), where the most parsimonious specifications rejected
(the two large July excise transitions, 4 and 28 July, briefly blip
the measured net price that the tax-stripped target leaves in the
error --- the deeper oil polynomial absorbs them). Lag selection is by
BIC over $p \in [0,10]$, $q \in [7,10]$; the same exhaustive grid
without the floor reproduces the \textsf{ARDL} package's
\texttt{auto\_ardl} selections.

\paragraph{Specification robustness.}
\Cref{tab:ardl-robust} re-runs the entire exercise --- lag selection,
backtest, projection and reference-day inflation --- under variants of
the main specification of \cref{sec:cfresults}, all selecting by BIC
with a maximum lag of ten: the oil-lag floor relaxed from the main
seven to five, two or none; a pure distributed lag with no
autoregressive term; and, as an economically misspecified reference, an
autoregression with no oil term at all. Petrol is remarkably flat
across the floors: its BIC order walks between $(5,0)$ and $(4,7)$,
the long-run elasticity stays between 0.43 and 0.46, and the mean gap
between $-16.2$ and $-16.5$~c/l. Diesel welcomes the main
specification's deeper oil lags: relaxing the floor degrades its
backtest from 4.01~c/l (seven lags) to 5.03~c/l (no floor), and its
gap spans $-24.3$ to $-27.5$~c/l. The no-oil reference is instructive
for the opposite reason: daily net prices are so persistent that it
fits almost as well in sample ($R^2$ 0.9985 versus 0.9988 for petrol)
--- exactly why in-sample fit cannot arbitrate this question --- yet it
forecasts worse out of sample (petrol RMSE 3.19 versus 1.93~c/l) and
understates the effect; excluding the Brent block is rejected with
$p < 10^{-4}$ in both fuels. Dropping the autoregressive dimension
entirely leaves petrol essentially unchanged but degrades diesel badly
(RMSE 11.8~c/l, worse than the random walk), whose multi-week margin
persistence only the autoregressive part carries. Across the
economically sensible specifications the October mean gaps stay within
14.6--16.5~c/l (petrol) and 24.3--27.7~c/l (diesel), and the combined
CPI effect within $-0.40$ to $-0.43$~percentage points.
